# Startup Failure Patterns

The causes of death that show up again and again in startup post-mortems, with early warning signs, real examples, and what to do instead.

## 1. How often each cause appears

CB Insights' 2021 analysis of 111 founder post-mortems (founders could cite more than one reason, so the numbers add up to more than 100%):

| Cause | Share of post-mortems |
|---|---|
| Ran out of cash / failed to raise | 38% |
| No market need | 35% |
| Got outcompeted | ~20% |
| Flawed business model | ~19% |
| Regulatory / legal challenges | ~18% |
| Pricing / cost issues | ~15% |
| Not the right team | ~14% |
| Product mistimed | ~10% |
| Poor product | ~8% |
| Disharmony among team / investors | ~7% |
| Pivot gone bad | ~6% |
| Burned out / lack of passion | 5% |

An earlier CB Insights study had "no market need" at 42% and "ran out of cash" at 29%. Either way, the top two are the same and dominate everything else.

CB Insights' own founder made the point that running out of cash is usually the *result* of another problem (no demand, bad economics, overspending), not the root cause. Treat it that way in audits.

Separately, the Startup Genome project's research on early-stage companies found premature scaling (spending and hiring ahead of proven demand) to be one of the most common patterns among startups that failed.

## 2. No market need

The product solves a problem that isn't painful, frequent, or important enough for people to pay or change behavior.

**Warning signs**
- Validation is compliments, survey answers, or friends and family, not money, deposits, signed letters of intent, or repeat usage.
- The target customer is "everyone", "small businesses", or "Gen Z" with no narrower first group.
- Months of building before talking to customers; launch planned as a big reveal.
- The founder can't name what customers do today instead, or "today they do nothing" (often means the problem isn't painful).
- Weak retention: people try it once and don't come back.
- The pitch leads with the technology instead of the problem.

**Examples**
- **Quibi**: raised about $1.75B for short, premium, phone-only shows; shut down roughly six months after its April 2020 launch. Huge spending did not create demand people hadn't shown.
- **Juicero**: a roughly $400 connected juicer backed by about $120M; in 2017 reporters showed its juice packs could be squeezed by hand just as well, and the company shut down that year.
- **Color Labs**: raised about $41M before launching a photo-sharing app in 2011 that users didn't adopt; wound down in 2012.

**What survivors do**: get commitment before building (pre-orders, paid pilots, a manual "concierge" version), pick one narrow customer group, and measure retention, not signups.

## 3. Ran out of cash (usually a symptom)

**Warning signs**
- Under 12 months of runway with fundraising not yet started; under 6 months is urgent.
- The plan depends on raising the next round on schedule, with no fallback.
- Monthly burn growing faster than revenue.
- Big fixed commitments (leases, salaries, inventory) made ahead of revenue.
- No one owns cash forecasting; the founders can't say their burn and runway quickly.

**Examples**
- **Fast** (one-click checkout): widely reported to have brought in only a few hundred thousand dollars of revenue in 2021 while burning around $10M a month; shut down in April 2022.
- **Zirtual** (virtual assistants): shut down suddenly in 2015 after burn outran its cash; the founder later described the failure as one of financial management.

**What survivors do**: start raising with 6+ months of cash left, keep a "cut plan" ready for if the round doesn't come, and tie each new spend to a milestone.

**In the audit**: always name the root cause behind the cash problem.

## 4. Broken unit economics / no business model

Each sale loses money, or the cost to get a customer is higher than what they'll ever pay.

**Warning signs**
- Price below cost to serve, with "we'll make it up at scale" as the plan.
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) unknown, or higher than lifetime value (LTV). A common healthy rule of thumb is LTV at least ~3x CAC.
- Low or negative gross margin, especially with heavy shipping, labor, or subsidies.
- Growth relies on discounts or paid ads that would stop working if turned off.
- Long-term costs locked in against short-term revenue.

**Examples**
- **Pets.com**: sold heavy, low-margin goods with expensive shipping, often below cost; liquidated in 2000.
- **MoviePass**: offered roughly unlimited movie tickets for about $10 a month in 2018 while paying theaters full price per ticket; its parent company went bankrupt in 2020.
- **WeWork**: signed long-term leases and rented space out short-term; its 2019 IPO collapsed and it filed for bankruptcy in 2023.

**What survivors do**: prove that one customer is profitable (or will be, with a clear path) before scaling, and test real prices early.

## 5. Premature scaling and overhiring

Spending to grow before the product clearly works.

**Warning signs**
- Hiring plan grows headcount before paying customers or retention exist.
- Sales and marketing hires before founders have sold the product themselves.
- Expanding to new cities, countries, or products before the first one works.
- Big office, brand campaigns, or events at the pre-product-market-fit stage.
- Payroll is most of the burn and grows every month regardless of revenue.

**Examples**
- **Webvan**: built expensive automated warehouses and expanded to multiple cities before demand caught up; bankrupt in 2001.
- **Katerra**: construction-tech company that raised billions, expanded rapidly into many parts of construction at once, and filed for bankruptcy in 2021.

**What survivors do**: hire in response to demand ("we're turning customers away") rather than in anticipation of it, and expand only after the first market is clearly working.

## 6. Wrong team and co-founder conflict

**Warning signs**
- A critical skill is missing from the founders: no technical founder for a software product, no one who has sold for a sales-led product, no domain expert in a regulated industry.
- Key work outsourced (e.g., the core product built entirely by an agency) with no one in-house who understands it.
- Founders' commitment is unequal (one full-time, one "will join after funding").
- No vesting schedule; equity split chosen without discussion; no agreement on who decides what.
- Many co-founders (4+) with overlapping roles.
- A board or advisors who look impressive but don't know the industry.

**Examples**
- **Theranos**: its board was full of prominent political and military figures with little medical or lab-science expertise, which made it easier for problems to go unchallenged. (Also see pattern 13.)

**What survivors do**: fill critical gaps with a founder or early hire, put standard 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff on all founders, and write down roles and decision rights early.

## 7. Outcompeted

**Warning signs**
- "We have no competitors."
- Competitor list ignores the status quo (spreadsheets, WhatsApp, doing nothing) and big companies that could add this as a feature.
- The advantage is something easy to copy (a feature, a lower price, a nicer design).
- A much better-funded competitor is in the same market with the same approach.

**Examples**
- **Rdio**: a well-liked music streaming service that lost to Spotify on growth and marketing; filed for bankruptcy in 2015 and its assets went to Pandora.
- **Wesabe**: personal finance tool that lost to Mint; its founder's post-mortem said Mint was simply easier to use.
- **Jawbone**: wearables and audio company squeezed by Fitbit and Apple; went into liquidation in 2017 after raising very large sums.

**What survivors do**: compete on something hard to copy (a specific customer group, distribution, data, network effects) or go where big players won't bother.

## 8. Platform or single-customer dependency

**Warning signs**
- Most traffic or users come from one platform (an app store, a social network, one marketplace, one search engine, one AI model provider) that could change its rules.
- One customer is a large share of revenue.
- Core function depends on another company's API with no contract.

**Example**
- **Meerkat**: a live-streaming app that grew on Twitter; Twitter cut off its access and launched its own rival, Periscope, and Meerkat shut down in 2016 (the team pivoted to a new app).

**What survivors do**: build a direct relationship with users (email, accounts), diversify channels, and get contracts for critical dependencies.

## 9. Poor or overbuilt product

**Warning signs**
- First version has many features and a long build time; launch keeps slipping.
- The product needs a lot of explanation or setup before the user gets value.
- Promises on the website or in the pitch the product doesn't yet deliver.
- No feedback loop: no usage data, no regular customer conversations.

**Examples**
- **Humane AI Pin**: a heavily funded wearable AI device that launched in 2024 to poor reviews about speed, battery and usefulness; HP bought the company's assets in early 2025 and the devices were shut down.
- **Juicero** (see pattern 2) is also an example of overbuilt hardware.

**What survivors do**: ship the smallest version that delivers the core value, watch real users, and cut features that don't get used.

## 10. Bad timing

**Warning signs**
- Depends on customer behavior or infrastructure that doesn't exist yet at scale.
- Arriving after the market has consolidated around winners.
- No answer to "why now?" (what changed in technology, regulation, or behavior that makes this possible today?).

**Examples**
- **Webvan** (see pattern 5) was early: online grocery later worked for others once smartphones, gig labor, and consumer habits had changed.
- **General Magic** built smartphone-like devices in the 1990s, long before the networks and components needed for them.

**What survivors do**: articulate a concrete "why now" and, if early, keep costs low enough to wait for the market.

## 11. Lost focus and bad pivots

**Warning signs**
- Several products, customer groups, or markets at once at the early stage.
- Frequent pivots without data showing why.
- Revenue from custom one-off work pulling the team away from the product.

**Example**
- **Fab.com**: pivoted from a social network to flash sales to a design-goods store, burning through large amounts of funding during rapid expansion; it was sold for a small fraction of its peak valuation in 2015.

**What survivors do**: pick one customer and one problem, set a time-boxed test with a clear success metric, and pivot only on evidence.

## 12. Legal, regulatory and compliance

**Warning signs**
- Industry needs a license (finance, health, insurance, transport, food, alcohol, education for minors, real estate) and the plan doesn't mention it.
- Handles sensitive data (health, financial, children's, biometric) with no privacy plan or privacy policy.
- Workers treated as contractors while being controlled like employees.
- Unclear IP ownership (code written by freelancers or co-founders without assignment agreements).
- Marketing claims that regulators police (health outcomes, guaranteed returns, "AI-powered" when it isn't).

**Examples**
- **Homejoy** (home cleaning marketplace): shut down in 2015; lawsuits over classifying its cleaners as contractors were one of the reasons its founder cited, alongside poor customer retention.

**What survivors do**: get a short legal review for their industry early, file IP assignment agreements for everyone who builds, and keep claims within what can be proven.

## 13. Integrity failures: faked traction, fraud, overclaiming

This one is usually fatal and often criminal. Flag it plainly.

**Warning signs**
- Traction numbers that don't add up or can't be verified.
- "AI" or "automated" claims for work actually done manually, without disclosure.
- Customer logos or testimonials that look invented.
- Mixing company money with personal or related-company money.
- Pressure to hide problems from investors or the board.

**Examples**
- **Theranos**: claimed its blood-testing technology worked when it did not; the company dissolved in 2018 and its founder was convicted of fraud in 2022.
- **FTX**: the crypto exchange collapsed in November 2022 after customer funds were found to have been misused; its founder was convicted of fraud in 2023.
- **Builder.ai**: a heavily funded "AI" app-building company that entered insolvency in 2025 after its revenue figures were found to be overstated.

**What survivors do**: report honest numbers, describe manual work honestly ("concierge" phases are fine when disclosed), and keep clean books from day one.

## 14. Founder burnout and lost conviction

**Warning signs**
- One or two founders covering every function with no plan to hand anything off.
- Founder working a full-time job plus the startup indefinitely.
- Founder motivation is mainly about fundraising, status, or a trend rather than the problem.

**Example**
- **Secret** (anonymous sharing app): its founder shut it down in 2015, saying the product no longer reflected his vision for the company, and returned remaining money to investors.

**What survivors do**: plan the first hires around removing founder bottlenecks, and pick a problem the founders will still care about in five years.

## 15. Capital-heavy and hardware traps

**Warning signs**
- Inventory must be bought months before sales.
- Manufacturing, certification, or vehicles/real estate needed before revenue.
- Plan needs several large funding rounds to reach a working product.

**Examples**
- **Pebble**: a popular smartwatch maker that struggled with funding and competition; it sold assets to Fitbit in 2016.
- **Argo AI**: self-driving company that raised billions and was shut down by its main backers, Ford and Volkswagen, in 2022.
- **Cruise**: GM's self-driving unit, which raised well over $10B; GM stopped funding its robotaxi work at the end of 2024.

**What survivors do**: pre-sell (crowdfunding, deposits), outsource manufacturing, or find a less capital-heavy first product.
